# Owning a car in Argentina: mandatory insurance, inspection, licence, fines (general)

Written 2026-10-20. Sources: the text printed on a mandatory-insurance premium receipt (pasted by a user), news and agency pages found by search. **The laws themselves were not read**; verify anything before acting. Cross-links: `TRAFFIC_FINES.md`, `CASH_CRUNCH_PLAYBOOK.md` s.13, `QUICK_INCOME_PLAYBOOK.md`.

## 1. Mandatory third-party insurance (the minimum cover)

- The receipt of the compulsory policy cites its legal basis: the national traffic law (Ley 24.449, art. 68, as amended by Ley 26.363) and its regulation, **Decreto 1716/08**. It says possessing the receipt is **sufficient proof that the insurance is in force**.
- It also cites **Disposicion 70/2009 of the national road-safety agency (art. 2)**: the **driver not carrying the premium receipt cannot be used by the authority as proof of non-compliance** with the requirements to circulate. In practice the authority can check the policy in the database; carrying the receipt helps but is not what makes the car legal.
- The same receipt lists **what to do after an accident**: collect the other driver's name, address, phone and licence; the other car's make, plate, owner and insurer; the names, addresses and phones of witnesses; then contact the insurance producer or the company. Insurers often add a **legal-assistance phone line for accidents with injured third parties**: keep that number with the policy.
- **Direct debit:** many policies are charged by direct debit (the receipt says so when it is). Check that the account has funds on the debit date: a failed debit can lapse the cover.
- Why a car that stands still still needs cover (the owner's reasoning, plausible): a car can roll, be hit by a storm or a falling tree and damage others. The owner answers for the damage; without insurance a claim becomes a long dispute even if finally won.
- **Not confirmed:** whether the obligation applies to a vehicle that does not circulate at all; one source says there are specific covers for cars kept in a garage (theft, fire) and that third-party cover is required again when the car circulates. Ask the insurer about suspension and reactivation **without a new inspection** before cancelling anything (an expired inspection can block a new policy: `CASH_CRUNCH_PLAYBOOK.md` s.13).

### Reading a policy front page (what to look for)

- **The plan**: a basic policy is "civil liability" (third-party damage) with a limit; the label (a letter and a number) is the insurer's own. Anything beyond third-party (theft, fire, own damage) must be listed.
- **Validity versus billed period**: a policy may run a year while the premium is billed in six-month endorsements, each with its own price (the next one is usually dearer).
- **The breakdown of the premium**: the net premium, VAT, a stamp tax, other taxes and fees, and, when you pay in instalments with a card, a **financing charge** (an annual effective rate is printed): in the example read it came to about 4% of the endorsement before its VAT. Paying the half-year in one go would avoid it, at the cost of cash now.
- **Payment plan**: dates and amounts per instalment, and the means (a card or a bank account). A card plan consumes card limit and fails if the card is blocked or full.
- **Article 12 of the insurance law, printed on the policy**: if the policy text differs from your proposal, the difference is deemed approved unless you claim **within one month** of receiving it. Read the policy in that month.
- **Who to call**: the insurer's customer-service line and, if a claim is not solved, the national insurance regulator (its phone and email are printed on the policy).

## 2. Technical inspection (VTV)

- Province of Buenos Aires: reported fee of about 97,000 pesos for a car up to 2,500 kg since January 2026 (verify the fee in force); some owners have a lower fee for old vehicles; fee exemptions exist for low-income retirees and holders of a disability certificate; the inspection is still required. Frequency in the province: annual after the first two years (one source; verify).
- Circulating with an expired inspection is an infraction with no grace period, and an insurer may refuse a claim (press; read the policy). Some people report that showing proof of a booked appointment is tolerated when stopped: treat it as a **tolerance, not a right**; keep the proof printed and drive only the direct route to the plant.

## 3. Driving licence

Class B1 covers ordinary cars; professional work (delivery by car, taxi, trucks) uses other classes with different requirements and fees (`QUICK_INCOME_PLAYBOOK.md`). An unpaid fine can block a licence renewal (secondary source).

## 5. A one-page checklist for an owner

1. Insurance in force, direct debit funded, the accident checklist and the assistance number on paper in the glovebox.
2. Inspection date known, appointment booked before expiry; the fee budgeted.
3. Licence expiry known (and the renewal not blocked by fines).
4. Plate checked in every jurisdiction every couple of months (`TRAFFIC_FINES.md`).
5. If the car will not be used for a long time: ask the insurer in writing about suspension, a stored-vehicle cover and reactivation without inspection; compare with selling it (check fines first).

## 5b. Keep the addresses straight

- The **insurer's risk address** (where the vehicle is kept, garage or street) and the **registered address of the vehicle** can differ from where the owner really lives. The premium often depends on the first, and official notices (fines, inspection reminders) go to the second. If they differ, tell the insurer what address they need, and ask how notices reach you; changing the registered address is a procedure at the automotive registry (cost and steps to look up).
- A fine notice sent to an old address can pass unseen and the defence deadline can expire: check the plate in every jurisdiction every couple of months (`TRAFFIC_FINES.md`).
